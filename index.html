Australian Employment and Unemployment · 2016–2026

Who is finding work in Australia, and who is not?

Australia's jobless rate has fallen from 5.6% to 4.4% in a decade. But that one number hides a lot: some places, ages and groups of workers are doing far better than others. Scroll down to see where the gaps are.

1 · Where

Where is unemployment highest?

Australia is divided here into 87 regions (called SA4s by the Australian Bureau of Statistics). Each region is coloured by its unemployment rate: the share of people who want to work but cannot find a job. Pale yellow means few people are out of work and dark red means many. Use the year buttons to move between 2016, 2021 and 2026, and hover over a region for its exact rate.

What it shows: The map got paler between 2016 and 2026 almost everywhere. But in 2026 the rate still ranges from 2.2% in Sydney – Sutherland to 7.3% in the Queensland Outback, so where you live still matters a lot.

2 · Where, up close

Where do the unemploymed actually live?

A high rate in a huge outback region sounds alarming, but few people live there. These close-ups zoom in on each state and colour regions by the number of people unemployed instead. Darker means more people, so big cities stand out.

Under 3,000 3,000–6,000 6,000–10,000 10,000–15,000 15,000–25,000 25,000 or more

What it shows: The outback has the highest rates but the fewest people. The darkest regions are in the cities. Melbourne – West alone has about 36,000 unemployed people, and the five biggest regions together hold about one in five of all unemployed Australians.

3 · Change over time

Has the jobs picture improved since 2016?

Each small map compares one year with 2016. Blue regions have a lower unemployment rate than in 2016 (good news) and red regions have a higher one. Read the maps from left to right to follow the story from 2018 to 2026.

What it shows: The first two panels are mixed, and in 2020 the pandemic pushed many regions' rates above their 2016 level. From 2022 blue takes over, and by 2026 81 of the 87 regions are better off than in 2016. Perth – South West and Wide Bay improved the most, each falling by about 3 percentage points. The exceptions are Hobart, Darwin and the Northern Territory Outback, where rates have risen.

4 · Gender

Who finds work harder, women or men?

Each tile is one state or territory, placed roughly where it sits on the map so every state gets the same space. The colour shows the gap between the women's and men's unemployment rates in July 2026. Teal means the rate is higher for men, purple means it is higher for women, and grey means about equal.

What it shows: Nationally the two rates are almost identical (4.4% for women, 4.5% for men), but the states are not. Tasmania has the biggest gap, with men's unemployment at 6.2% against 3.9% for women. Western Australia is the reverse, with women at 5.0% and men at 3.8%.

5 · Age

Which age groups find work hardest?

Each row is an age group and each column is a year (every July). Each cell is coloured by the unemployment rate, from pale yellow (low) to dark red (high), and the number inside is the exact rate. The bottom row shows all ages together for comparison.

What it shows: The top row is red in every year. Young people (15–24) were hit hardest in 2020, when 15.9% were unemployed, and in 2026 the rate is still 10.1%, more than three times the rate for people in their 30s, 40s and 50s. Older workers barely changed, even in 2020.

6 · Age and gender together

Who are the unemployed?

This pyramid counts people rather than rates. Each bar is an age group, with men extending to the left and women to the right. The longer the bar, the more people in that group are out of work.

What it shows: The pyramid is widest at the bottom. About 137,000 men and 117,000 women aged 15–24 are unemployed, which is more than a third of all unemployed people. Between 35 and 54, women outnumber men.

7 · Over time

How has the make-up of the unemployed changed?

This stream graph shows the number of unemployed people every month from 2016 to 2026. Each coloured band is an age group, and the band's width is how many people in that group are unemployed. The red band is the youngest group (15–24). Hover over any band to see the month and the number of people.

What it shows: The whole stream swells when COVID-19 hit in 2020, from about 816,000 unemployed people in January 2016 to a peak of about 982,000 in July 2020. It then narrows to about 681,000 by July 2026. The red band stays at around 37% of the total throughout, so young people have been over a third of the unemployed in almost every year. The thin 65+ band at the bottom is growing slowly, from about 6,000 people to about 23,000.

8 · Ten years on

Which states have come furthest in ten years?

Each line joins one state's unemployment rate in July 2016 (left) to its rate in July 2026 (right). A line sloping down means unemployment fell. Blue lines fell, red lines rose, and the dashed grey line is Australia as a whole. The steeper the line, the bigger the change. Hover over a line for the exact numbers.

What it shows: Most states have fallen a long way. South Australia (6.3% to 4.1%), Western Australia (6.5% to 4.4%) and Queensland (6.0% to 4.2%) improved the most, each by about 2 percentage points or more. Tasmania and Victoria barely moved, staying near 5%. The Northern Territory is the only place where unemployment clearly rose, from 3.4% to 4.6%.

9 · Ranking

Who has been best and worst at keeping people in work?

This chart ranks the eight states and territories from 1 (lowest unemployment rate) to 8 (highest) for every July from 2016 to 2026. Each coloured line follows one state, and the number inside each dot is its rank that year. Hover over a dot to follow that state and see its exact unemployment rate.

What it shows: The rankings change a lot from year to year. Western Australia went from last in 2016 to first in 2024, and South Australia climbed from 7th to 2nd. Victoria was first in 2022 but has been last since 2024. Tasmania was first in 2025 and then dropped to 7th, and the ACT has finished first in six of the last ten years.
